**For twenty-four years Tulika Prakash has worked inside the law. Now she is carrying it into the places it rarely reaches: the family home, the prayer room, the confidence whispered to the wrong person.**

There is a particular silence that follows a betrayal of trust. Tulika Prakash has spent a career learning to hear it.

As an **Advocate-on-Record at the Supreme Court of India**, she has argued civil and criminal matters, family and consumer disputes, contracts and arbitration. But ask what occupies her now and she speaks less about the courtroom than about the stories that never get there: the ones told in confidence, to the people least worthy of it.

## Institutions that were trusted and failed

Her recent public work reads like a list of institutions that were trusted and failed. She has turned her attention to gangs that groom boys, a danger society still prefers not to name. To religious subterfuge, where spiritual authority becomes a costume for manipulation. To the "ideal family" stratagem, in which a flawless surface covers what happens behind it. Her answer in each case is the same: expose it, name it, and refuse the comfort of looking away.

> "Har dard bayaan karna." Give voice to every pain.

It is how she frames the task, and behind it sit harder questions. What is right, and why, and who decides? Which human virtues have we quietly let go of? Her conclusion is that social and family problems demand a fresh approach if law and justice are to prevail at all.

## Guarding trust as fiercely as life

Nowhere is that clearer than in a story she tells from real life: a woman who confided her thoughts of suicide to a religious mentor, and saw that confidence betrayed. For Prakash the lesson is larger than one breach. It is the absence of any structure to protect people at their most exposed. She argues for **ethical, innovative suicide prevention**, built on the principle that trust must be guarded as fiercely as life itself, in a world changing faster than its safeguards.

## Recognition

The recognition arriving now reflects that direction. In 2025 she was named among the **Top 30 Women Achievers**, awarded for leadership and innovation by a leading pan-Indian organisation committed to the mental health and emotional wellness of women. It is a fitting honour: it speaks to the very ground her work now covers. She is also recognised by **Forbes**, and describes herself, simply and without apology, as "The Advocate."

## Loyal to oneself

The conviction underneath is personal. "Being loyal to oneself is the greatest gift a woman will ever give herself," she says, "and no one teaches her this." It means living in one's truth, without owing every person a share in it. Privacy, in her view, is a fundamental right and a human one, and it begins early. Even a child deserves to say no.

Her method is as legal as her cause. She does not proceed on assumptions, presumptions, conjectures or surmises, an approach, she notes, that the law itself does not permit. Only The Advocate knows what The Advocate must do.

## What comes next

What comes next is a practice built to prevent as well as to prosecute: one that listens earlier, protects confidences, and treats dignity as a legal question rather than a sentimental one. Her platforms, from **tulutalks_official** to her public speaking, will carry that argument to people long before they ever need a courtroom.

She has a taste for the declarative, and a sign-off to match. *I came. I saw. And I destroyed everyone.* It is delivered, one suspects, with a smile. She calls herself the **Advocator Dominator**. Those on the wrong side of the truth may find the title less playful than it sounds.
